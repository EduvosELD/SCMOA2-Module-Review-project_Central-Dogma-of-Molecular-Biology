The Central Dogma of Molecular Biology

The central dogma of molecular biology describes the flow of genetic information within a cell, where DNA serves as the stable storage molecule, RNA acts as an intermediate messenger, and proteins are produced as functional products. This concept explains how genetic instructions are expressed and how cells use inherited information to produce the molecules needed for structure, function, and regulation.

Central Dogma of Molecular Biology Explained
The Cell

The cell acts as a protective container for an organism's master blueprint: DNA

•  In eukaryotic cells (like human, plant, or animal cells), DNA is tightly packed and sequestered inside the nucleus, protecting it from damaging chemical reactions occurring in the rest of the cell

•  In prokaryotic cells (like bacteria), though they lack a nucleus, the cell confines the DNA to a specific region called the nucleoid

Transcription

Eukaryotic protein-coding genes

RNA polymerase II transcribes a protein-coding gene’s DNA sequence into a complementary pre-mRNA transcript within the nucleus.

  • Transcription factors bind to the promoter and recruit RNA polymerase II. The DNA is locally unwound, exposing the template strand.
  • RNA polymerase II reads the template strand 3′→5′ and synthesises pre-mRNA 5′→3′ through complementary base pairing. In RNA, uracil (U) replaces thymine (T).
  • After the polyadenylation signal has been transcribed, the pre-mRNA is cleaved downstream of this signal, and RNA polymerase II eventually detaches from the DNA.
  • The pre-mRNA is processed by the addition of a 5′ cap, removal of introns and joining of exons, and addition of a poly-A tail.
  • The mature mRNA, which contains both coding regions and non-coding untranslated regions, is exported from the nucleus to the cytoplasm for translation.

Prokaryotic protein-coding genes

RNA polymerase transcribes a protein-coding gene’s DNA sequence into a complementary mRNA transcript within the cytoplasm.

  • The RNA polymerase holoenzyme, containing a sigma factor, recognises and binds to the promoter. The DNA is locally unwound, exposing the template strand.
  • RNA polymerase reads the template strand 3′→5′ and synthesises mRNA 5′→3′ through complementary base pairing. In RNA, uracil (U) replaces thymine (T).
  • Transcription ends through either intrinsic termination or Rho-dependent termination, causing the RNA transcript and RNA polymerase to be released.
  • Prokaryotic mRNA generally undergoes little processing and may contain the coding sequences of several genes in a single polycistronic transcript.
  • Because prokaryotic cells lack a nucleus, ribosomes can begin translating the mRNA while transcription is still occurring.

Translation

Translation is how ribosomes read the codons in mRNA and assemble a matching chain of amino acids — using tRNA as the adaptor between the nucleic-acid code and the amino-acid code — to build a protein.

•  The ribosome assembles on the mRNA at the start codon, AUG (coding for methionine), which sets the reading frame

•  mRNA is read in triplets called codons; each codon specifies one amino acid — or a stop signal — according to the genetic code

•  A transfer RNA (tRNA) carrying a matching anticodon delivers the correct amino acid into the ribosome, base-pairing with the codon

•  A peptide bond links the new amino acid to the growing chain, and the ribosome shifts one codon along the mRNA (translocation)

•  Elongation repeats until a stop codon (UAA, UAG or UGA) is reached — no tRNA matches it, so the finished polypeptide is released and folds into a functional protein

Beyond the Dogma: Exceptions & Nuances

The "one-way" DNA → RNA → protein flow taught above is the simplified textbook version. Several real biological processes reveal a more flexible picture — though, as the diagram shows, none of them actually break Francis Crick's original rule.

•  Reverse transcription (RNA→DNA): retroviruses like HIV use the enzyme reverse transcriptase to copy their RNA genome into DNA, which then integrates into the host cell's own genome

•  RNA replication (RNA→RNA): many RNA viruses (e.g. influenza, coronaviruses) copy their RNA genome directly into new RNA using RNA-dependent RNA polymerase — no DNA step involved

•  Prions: infectious misfolded proteins that trigger other proteins to misfold — often cited as an "exception," though no genetic information is actually being copied here

•  The one rule that never breaks: Crick's 1958 Central Dogma only forbids one direction — information can never flow from protein back into DNA or RNA. Every process above, however unusual, still respects it
